# Conflict Resolution

🎯 Interview Weight: critical - conflict resolution is
assessed at every level; inability to resolve interpersonal
or technical conflict constructively is a disqualifier
for senior+ roles and a yellow flag at junior levels.

---

### 🎯 Model Answer

**30 seconds:**
> Conflict resolution means addressing disagreements
> directly and constructively, with the goal of finding
> a path forward rather than winning the argument.
> In engineering, most conflicts are about technical
> decisions, resource priorities, or working style
> differences. The key behaviors: address early before
> it compounds, lead with the shared goal, separate
> the person from the position, and look for a forward
> path - even if it is not your preferred path.

**3 minutes (Senior):**
> Engineering conflicts fall into two categories:
> technical disagreements (where there is a correctness
> dimension) and interpersonal conflicts (where there
> is a relationship dimension). Each needs a different
> approach.
>
> Technical disagreements: use data and explicit
> criteria. Define what a good solution looks like,
> apply the criteria to both positions, let the
> criteria decide. The framing that defuses most
> technical conflicts: "We both want [shared goal].
> The disagreement is about how to get there. Can
> we define what criteria we'd use to evaluate
> the options?" Once criteria are shared, the
> disagreement becomes a test of options against
> criteria, not a clash of opinions.
>
> Interpersonal conflicts: address directly but
> privately. Letting interpersonal friction compound
> by not addressing it is the most common mistake.
> The pattern that works: describe the specific
> behavior and its impact (SBI), ask for the other
> person's perspective, look for a working agreement
> that adjusts the dynamic.
>
> For both: disagree and commit. Once a decision is
> made, even if you opposed it, you commit to it
> fully. Half-hearted commitment poisons team
> dynamics. The phrase to use: "I disagree with
> this approach for [specific reasons], and I
> commit to it. I'd like to revisit at [milestone]
> if [condition] occurs."

**Framework:** WHAT -> WHY -> HOW -> TRADE-OFF -> EXAMPLE

*Adapting up:* Senior engineers create conflict
resolution structures for the team: how are
technical disagreements escalated, who has final
say on architectural decisions, how are priority
conflicts between teams resolved. The structure
prevents escalation of routine disagreements.

*Adapting down:* "Address disagreements directly and
early, before they compound. Lead with the shared
goal. Separate the person from the position.
Be willing to disagree and commit once a decision
is made."

**Blank Mind Recovery:**

**(1) Restate:** "You are asking about conflict resolution -
let me walk through what constructive conflict
handling looks like in engineering."

**(2) First principles:** "Teams have disagreements
because members have different information, different
values, or different mental models. Resolution means
aligning on one or more of these. Avoidance lets the
underlying misalignment persist and compound."

**(3) Bridge:** "Technical conflict is like a race
condition in distributed systems: two processes have
different views of the state and act on their
respective views, creating inconsistency. Resolution
is synchronization - getting both processes to a
shared view."

### 📘 Concept Explanation

**What it is:**
Conflict resolution in engineering is the practice
of addressing disagreements directly, constructively,
and with the goal of finding a path forward that
the team can commit to - whether that is a mutually
acceptable solution or a decision made by the
appropriate authority.

**The problem it solves:**
Unresolved conflict creates hidden work: passive
disagreement with decisions, fragmented execution
on a project, and team members spending social
energy managing the conflict rather than doing
engineering work. Constructive resolution makes
the conflict visible, processes it, and returns
the team to aligned execution.

**How it works:**

```
CONFLICT RESOLUTION FRAMEWORK
================================

STEP 1: IDENTIFY THE CONFLICT TYPE
  Technical: disagreement about correctness or approach
    -> Use criteria and data (see Keyword 2)
  Priority: different views on what matters most
    -> Escalate to decision authority with options
  Interpersonal: working style or relationship friction
    -> Address 1:1 using SBI + forward agreement

STEP 2: ADDRESS EARLY
  Conflict compounds: the longer it waits,
  the more context and emotion it accumulates

STEP 3: LEAD WITH SHARED GOAL
  "We both want [X]. The disagreement is about
   [specific aspect of how to get there]."
  This reframes conflict as a navigation problem,
  not a competition

STEP 4: SEPARATE PERSON FROM POSITION
  "I disagree with this approach" != "You are wrong"
  Critique the design, not the designer

STEP 5: DISAGREE AND COMMIT
  If resolution is not possible, accept the decision
  made by the appropriate authority
  Commit fully: do not hedge in execution
  Reserve: ask to revisit at a milestone

WHAT ESCALATION LOOKS LIKE
  Before escalating: "I want to resolve this
    at our level. I've tried [X] and [Y].
    Can we try [Z] before escalating?"
  When escalating: bring both positions,
    both sets of trade-offs, a recommended
    resolution. Do not escalate the conflict
    without bringing a proposed resolution.
```

> **Code walkthrough:** This example demonstrates the core pattern in action. The key mechanism shows how the concept works in practice. Study the structure to understand the essential behavior and common usage.

**The key insight:**
The quality of conflict resolution determines the
team's learning rate. Teams that resolve conflicts
constructively and extract the reasoning do not
repeat them. Teams that resolve conflicts by
avoidance or authority-decree without reasoning
continue to generate the same conflicts.

**When to use it:**
Any time you identify disagreement or friction.
The earlier you address it, the less it costs.
Conflict is not a failure state - it is expected
in any team doing complex work. The failure mode
is avoidance, not the conflict itself.

**When NOT to use it:**
Do not attempt peer conflict resolution for
misconduct, harassment, or ethical violations.
These require HR and management involvement,
not a 1:1 conversation.

**Alternatives:**
- Escalation to management: appropriate for
  priority conflicts between teams
- Third-party mediation: for persistent
  interpersonal conflicts
- Explicit team agreements: prevent conflicts
  by establishing norms before they are needed

**First-principles derivation:**
Complex teams have complex dependencies. Complex
dependencies generate disagreements. Disagreements
unresolved become compounding costs. Constructive
conflict resolution converts disagreements from
costs into learning and alignment.

### 🎓 Answers by Seniority

**Junior / Mid (0-5 years):**
> Address conflict early and directly. Lead with the
> shared goal. Be willing to disagree and commit once
> a decision is made. The habit that matters most:
> do not wait for someone else to resolve a conflict
> you are part of.

*Push deeper:* "The most difficult conflict for junior
engineers is disagreeing with a more senior person.
The approach: use data and criteria, not authority
appeals. 'I want to understand the trade-off here.
My concern is [specific technical issue]. What am
I missing in your evaluation?' This positions the
challenge as a learning question, not a challenge
to authority."

---

**Senior / Staff (5+ years):**
> Senior engineers both resolve conflicts and prevent
> them by designing team structures that make conflict
> less likely: clear decision ownership, explicit
> criteria for evaluating technical options, and
> escalation paths that are understood before they
> are needed.

The staff-level resolution skill is resolving conflicts
between teams, which requires representing your own
team's constraints clearly, understanding the other
team's constraints, and finding a path that each
team can commit to.

*Push deeper:* "The hardest conflicts for senior
engineers are disagreements with their manager or
skip-level on technical direction. The approach:
write the case, be specific about trade-offs, propose
two or three options with recommendations, and accept
the outcome. Losing a technical debate to a manager
who has more organizational context than you is
not always wrong - sometimes they know things you
do not."

### ⚠️ Common Misconceptions

**Misconception 1: Resolving conflict means reaching
consensus.**

Some conflicts should end in disagreement-with-commitment:
team members retain different views but agree to support
the chosen direction. The goal is forward progress with
relationships intact, not unanimous agreement. Forcing
false consensus produces resentment; productive
disagreement produces alignment and faster execution.

**Misconception 2: Avoiding conflict is professional -
raising issues causes tension.**

Unaddressed disagreements accumulate. A missed code review
comment about a design flaw becomes a production incident
three months later. Naming tensions early, professionally,
and with solution intent is a professional skill - not
aggression. Silence is not neutrality; it is deferred cost.

---

### 🚨 Failure Modes and Diagnosis

**Failure Mode 1: Conflict story where you won and the
other person was wrong.**

Symptom: answer ends with "I was right - the data proved
it." Evaluators assess whether you navigate conflict
constructively, not whether you win arguments. Fix:
reframe the outcome around joint decisions and the
relationship post-resolution. Even if technically correct,
lead with: "We reached alignment on X, which required
both of us to..."

**Failure Mode 2: Escalating to management as the first
conflict resolution step.**

Symptom: "I raised it with my manager right away."
Evaluators think: escalation dependence, cannot resolve
peer conflicts independently. Fix: direct conversation
first, explicit disagreement-to-commitment second,
manager involvement only when genuinely stuck despite
multiple sincere attempts.

### 🎯 Interview Deep-Dive

#### Definition
- "Tell me about a time you had a conflict with
  a teammate. How did you resolve it?"
- "Describe a situation where you disagreed with
  your manager."

🗣️ "**S:** I had a persistent conflict with a peer
engineer over code review standards. They felt
my reviews were blocking; I felt their code was
under-tested.
**T:** The tension was affecting our sprint velocity
and our working relationship.
**A:** I asked for a 1:1, opened with the shared goal:
'We both want high-quality code and fast delivery.
I think we have different implicit standards that
are creating friction.' I proposed we explicitly
define our team's code quality standards and use
them as the review criteria. We spent 90 minutes
defining the standards together. Once they were
written, most of the friction disappeared because
we were both working from the same criteria.
**R:** Our review turnaround improved and the
friction ended. The standards doc became part
of our team onboarding."

#### Comparison
- "How does resolving technical conflict differ
  from resolving interpersonal conflict?"
- "When should you escalate a conflict vs resolve
  it yourself?"

🗣️ "Technical conflict has a correctness dimension -
one approach may genuinely be better given explicit
criteria. Interpersonal conflict is about the working
relationship - behavior, communication, or style.
Technical conflict is best resolved by defining
criteria and applying them. Interpersonal conflict
is best resolved by a direct 1:1 conversation using
SBI: describe the specific behavior and its impact,
invite the other person's perspective, agree on a
going-forward adjustment. Escalate when: you have
addressed the conflict directly and it persists
after a reasonable attempt; the conflict involves
competing team priorities that require management
decision; the conflict involves misconduct."

#### Scenario
- "Tell me about a time you had to advocate for
  your position against strong resistance."
- "Describe a situation where you resolved a
  conflict between two other team members."

🗣️ "**S:** Two engineers on my team had a persistent
conflict about who owned the API design for a shared
service. Each had strong opinions and the disagreement
was creating delays.
**T:** I was the tech lead, not the manager. I needed
to resolve this without using authority I did not have.
**A:** I facilitated a 90-minute session. I opened with
the constraint: we need an API contract in two days.
I separated the decision from the relationship by
framing it as 'what criteria should we use?' not
'who is right?' The engineers aligned on five criteria.
I then ran them through both proposed APIs against
the criteria. Option A scored higher on three of
five criteria. We adopted it.
**R:** Decision made in 90 minutes. The engineer whose
option was not chosen said the process felt fair. I
documented the criteria for future API decisions."

| Interviewer Type | Emphasis |
|---|---|
| Technical Panel | Lead with criteria-based resolution for technical conflicts |
| Hiring Manager | Show you address conflict directly, not avoid it |
| Bar Raiser | Show you created a structure, not just resolved one instance |
| Peer Engineer | Be honest that the conflict was real and the outcome cost something |

### ⚖️ Comparison

| Resolution Approach | Speed | Relationship Health | Works When |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Direct + SBI** | Medium | Good | Interpersonal or style conflict |
| Criteria-based | Medium | Good | Technical disagreement |
| Escalation to manager | Fast | Variable | Priority or resource conflict |
| Avoidance | Fast initially | Poor | Never - compounding cost |

**The deciding factor:**
Use direct + SBI for interpersonal. Use criteria-
based for technical. Escalate when the conflict
requires a decision authority above the individuals.
Never choose avoidance.

### 🔥 Field Q&A

#### Behavioral Scenarios

Q: Tell me about a time a conflict damaged a project
or relationship. What did you learn?

A: "I avoided addressing a conflict with a peer
who was regularly missing sprint commitments. I
told myself they were dealing with personal issues
and I covered for them. Three sprints later, the
project was six weeks behind and the team had lost
trust in our reliability. I had waited too long.
The lesson: addressing conflict early - even
uncomfortably - is a kindness. Avoidance converts
a small problem into a large one. I now address
performance concerns after two missed commitments,
not six."

Q: How do you handle a conflict where you are
genuinely wrong?

A: "I update my position. The hardest part is the
public update - especially in a meeting where I
have been defending a position. The language I use:
'I've been looking at this wrong. [Name's] point
about [specific evidence] changes my assessment.
The better approach is [updated conclusion].' This
models the behavior I want from others. The fastest
way to earn respect is to change your mind when
the evidence is clear."

Q: Tell me about a time you had to tell a peer that
their behavior was affecting the team.

A: "A senior engineer on my team consistently
interrupted others in design reviews - not
maliciously, but habitually. Two engineers had
started deferring to them by default, which was
reducing the quality of input. I asked for a 1:1
and used SBI: 'In the last three design reviews,
I've noticed you consistently complete other people's
sentences or restate their ideas before they finish
[behavior]. Two engineers have stopped offering their
own proposals [impact].' The engineer was not aware
of the pattern. We agreed on a cue I would give when
it happened again. The behavior improved significantly."

#### Candidate Mistakes

Q: What is the most common mistake in conflict
resolution stories?

**What NOT to say:** "There was a conflict but we
talked it out and everything was fine."

**Say instead:** Give the specifics: what was the
conflict, what did you specifically do, what was the
resistance, how did the resolution happen, what
changed measurably afterward.

Q: What do candidates get wrong about disagree
and commit?

**What NOT to say:** "I disagreed but I just
went along with it."

**Say instead:** "I explicitly stated my disagreement
with the reasoning: 'I disagree because [specific
concern]. I commit to the decision and I want to
revisit at [milestone] if [condition] arises.'"

Q: What is the avoidance trap?

**What NOT to say:** "I don't like conflict, so
I usually try to find a middle ground and move on."

**Say instead:** "I address disagreements directly
and early, because the compounding cost of avoidance
is higher than the short-term discomfort of direct
conversation."

Q: How do candidates mishandle the "difficult person"
question?

**What NOT to say:** "I had a colleague who was
very difficult to work with. They were negative
and never helped the team."

**Say instead:** "I had a working style mismatch
with a colleague. I addressed it directly with a
1:1, used SBI to describe the impact, and we
created a working agreement. The relationship
improved."

#### Questions to Ask the Interviewer

Q: "How does this team handle persistent technical
disagreements? Is there a clear decision ownership
structure?"

*Why:* Reveals whether the team has structures
that prevent conflicts from compounding.
*If asked back:* "I prefer having clear decision
ownership - knowing who has final say on different
decision types prevents repeated debates."

Q: "Can you tell me about a time the team had a
significant disagreement? How was it resolved?"

*Why:* Real stories reveal real culture better
than stated values.
*If asked back:* "I think conflict is healthy and
necessary in engineering teams. The goal is not
to avoid it but to resolve it constructively."

Q: "How are disagreements between engineers and
product handled when priorities conflict?"

*Why:* Reveals the PM-engineering relationship
and how priority conflicts are managed.
*If asked back:* "I prefer having a structured
decision process rather than letting PM or
engineering unilaterally win every conflict."

Q: "What does a healthy retrospective look like
on this team?"

*Why:* Teams with good retros have practiced
constructive conflict resolution.
*If asked back:* "The retros I've run best have
started with specific observations, not general
impressions. 'Three incidents had the same root
cause' is more actionable than 'we need to
improve quality.'"

# Navigating Disagreements with Data

🎯 Interview Weight: high - using data to resolve
technical disagreements is a senior engineering
competency; it distinguishes engineers who build
shared truth from engineers who have opinions.

---

### 🎯 Model Answer

**30 seconds:**
> Navigating disagreements with data means converting
> a conflict of opinions into a test against evidence.
> The structure: agree on the question you are answering,
> agree on what evidence would change each position,
> collect that evidence, and apply it. The goal is to
> build shared truth, not win the argument.

**3 minutes (Senior):**
> Data-driven disagreement navigation has three phases:
> alignment, collection, and application.
>
> Alignment: before collecting data, align on what
> question the data will answer and what result would
> change each person's position. "If the p99 latency
> is above 200ms, does that change your preference?"
> If the answer is no - if no data would change the
> person's position - the disagreement is not about
> facts but about values or priorities. That is a
> different conversation.
>
> Collection: run the experiment, measure the metric,
> analyze the production behavior. The data should be
> specific to the decision at hand: baseline vs
> candidate, not general benchmarks.
>
> Application: apply the data to the criteria agreed
> upfront. If the data is clear, the decision follows
> from the criteria. If the data is ambiguous, be
> explicit about the ambiguity and what additional
> signal would help.
>
> The non-obvious insight: the most valuable outcome
> of a data-driven disagreement is not the decision -
> it is the explicit criteria that the team now shares.
> Two teams that run an experiment and make a decision
> together have built a shared analytical framework
> they can reuse. This compounds over time.

**Framework:** WHAT -> WHY -> HOW -> TRADE-OFF -> EXAMPLE

*Adapting up:* Staff engineers create the measurement
infrastructure and culture that makes data-driven
decisions possible. If the team has no way to measure
latency or error rates, no amount of methodology
makes data-driven resolution possible. The staff
contribution is building the measurement foundation.

*Adapting down:* "When you disagree with a colleague,
ask: 'What data would help us decide?' Then collect
that data. The goal is to convert 'I think' vs 'I think'
into 'the measurement shows.'"

**Blank Mind Recovery:**

**(1) Restate:** "You are asking about using data to
navigate disagreements - let me walk through the
approach and where it breaks down."

**(2) First principles:** "Disagreements often exist
because two people have different information or
different models. Data is a shared reference that
both can evaluate. Data does not eliminate judgment,
but it shifts the conversation from competing claims
to competing interpretations of shared evidence."

**(3) Bridge:** "Data-driven disagreement navigation
is like a court of appeal: both parties present their
evidence, a shared criteria evaluates it, and the
outcome follows from the evidence, not from who
is more senior or more persuasive."

### 📘 Concept Explanation

**What it is:**
Using data to navigate disagreements means converting
opinion-based conflicts into evidence-based evaluations:
aligning on the question, agreeing on what evidence
matters, collecting it, and applying it to reach a
decision that both parties can accept.

**The problem it solves:**
Technical teams have opinions - about frameworks,
architectures, APIs, algorithms. Without shared
evidence, these opinions produce gridlock or resolution-
by-authority. Data-driven navigation builds shared
analytical ground that makes the decision transparent
and the reasoning replicable.

**How it works:**

```
DATA-DRIVEN DISAGREEMENT FRAMEWORK
=====================================

STEP 1: ALIGN ON THE QUESTION
  "What specific question will the data answer?"
  Not: "Is approach A or B better?" (too broad)
  Yes: "At 1000 concurrent users, does approach A
        have lower p99 latency than approach B?"

STEP 2: AGREE ON DECISION CRITERIA
  "What result would change your position?"
  This tests if the disagreement is factual
  (answerable with data) or value-based (not)

STEP 3: AGREE ON MEASUREMENT APPROACH
  Which metric? Under what conditions?
  Who runs the experiment?
  How long does it run?

STEP 4: COLLECT DATA
  Benchmark, A/B test, production metric,
  historical analysis - match to decision

STEP 5: APPLY CRITERIA
  "The data shows X. We agreed X would determine Y.
   The decision is Y."
  If data is ambiguous, state the ambiguity explicitly

STEP 6: DOCUMENT
  What was the question, what data was collected,
  what was decided, what would trigger a revisit
```

> **Code walkthrough:** This example demonstrates the core pattern in action. The key mechanism shows how the concept works in practice. Study the structure to understand the essential behavior and common usage.

**The key insight:**
Step 2 is the diagnostic step. If a person cannot
state what evidence would change their position,
the disagreement is not about data. It is about
values, preferences, or identity. Data cannot
resolve values disagreements - those require a
different conversation about priority and trade-offs.

**When to use it:**
Technical decisions where the outcome is measurable:
performance, reliability, scalability, maintainability.
Any decision where you can run an experiment or
analyze existing data.

**When NOT to use it:**
Values disagreements (what we should optimize for),
design preference disagreements (which API is more
readable), and speed decisions (when the cost of
measurement exceeds the cost of being wrong). Not
everything can or should be data-driven.

**Alternatives:**
- Authority-based: faster, less shared understanding
- Consensus: slower, builds commitment
- Reversible decision: proceed, measure, revisit
- Expert opinion: appropriate when data collection
  is prohibitive

**First-principles derivation:**
Disagreements in technical systems are often
disagreements about which model of the system is
correct. Data is empirical validation of models.
If two people have different models, data can
determine which model better predicts actual system
behavior. Therefore, data can resolve model-based
disagreements by distinguishing which model is
more predictive.

### ⚠️ Common Misconceptions

**Misconception 1: More data always resolves
technical disagreements.**

Data resolves factual disagreements. Most technical
disagreements include a values dimension - reliability
vs speed, developer experience vs operational simplicity -
that data alone cannot resolve. Recognizing which type
of disagreement you are in determines the right
resolution approach before proposing a measurement.

**Misconception 2: Proposing an A/B test always
demonstrates data-driven thinking.**

A/B tests require traffic, time, and instrumentation.
Suggesting one for a low-traffic feature, a one-time
migration, or a decision needed in 48 hours signals
poor problem-scoping. Propose experiments only when
they fit the decision timeline and existing constraints.

---

### 🚨 Failure Modes and Diagnosis

**Failure Mode 1: Using data selectively to win rather
than to find the best answer.**

Symptom: candidate presents only the metric supporting
their position. Experienced reviewers ask "what does
[the other metric] say?" Fix: proactively present the
full picture including data that complicates your
position. "The throughput numbers favor my approach;
the P99 latency is slightly worse - here's my
reasoning about that trade-off."

**Failure Mode 2: Arguing from conclusions rather than
surfacing the underlying assumption.**

Symptom: conversation cycles because each party argues
from their conclusion. Fix: ask "what would have to be
true for your position to be right?" and "what would
have to be true for mine to be wrong?" This surfaces
the assumption difference, which is usually factual
and testable.

### 🎯 Interview Deep-Dive

#### Definition
- "How do you approach technical disagreements
  with a colleague who has more experience?"
- "Tell me about a time you used data to resolve
  a technical debate."

🗣️ "I always start by identifying what question
the data would answer and what result would change
each person's position. If both positions are
falsifiable with data, I propose the measurement.
If one position is not falsifiable (no evidence
would change it), the disagreement is about values,
not facts, and data alone will not resolve it.
The data tells us what is true about the system;
values conversations tell us what we should optimize
for."

#### Comparison
- "When should you use data to resolve a disagreement
  vs making a judgment call?"
- "How do you handle a situation where the data
  is ambiguous?"

🗣️ "Use data when the outcome is measurable and the
measurement cost is less than the cost of being
wrong. Make a judgment call when data collection
is prohibitively slow or expensive relative to the
decision stakes. When data is ambiguous, I am
explicit about the ambiguity: 'The data shows X in
condition A and Y in condition B. My interpretation
is [Z] because [reasoning]. If you interpret it
differently, walk me through your model and we
will either agree on an interpretation or identify
what additional data would distinguish our models.'"

- "Tell me about a time data changed your position

strategy would reduce our database load by 40%.

approach.

had opinions.

against production-representative load for 24 hours
and measured cache hit rate, database query volume,
and p99 response time. We agreed upfront: whichever
approach achieved higher cache hit rate with lower
p99 latency wins.

### ⚖️ Comparison

| Decision Method | Alignment Quality | Speed | Works When |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Data-driven** | High | Slow | Measurable outcomes available |
| Authority-based | Low | Fast | Speed critical, reversible |
| Consensus | High | Slow | Commitment needed, complex values |
| Expert judgment | Variable | Fast | Data collection prohibitive |

**The deciding factor:**
Use data when measurement is feasible and the
decision has material impact. Use authority when
speed matters and the decision is reversible.
Use consensus when team commitment to the outcome
is as important as the outcome itself.

### 🔥 Field Q&A

#### Behavioral Scenarios

Q: Tell me about a time you ran an experiment to
resolve a technical debate.

A: "Our team debated for two weeks whether to use
a push or pull architecture for a notification system.
I wrote a one-page test plan: we would implement
both approaches as prototypes, measure throughput,
latency under load, and implementation complexity
(lines of code, test coverage). We ran it for three
days. The pull approach was clearly superior on
throughput and complexity. We adopted it and the
two weeks of debate ended in one meeting. The lesson:
prototype races are faster than opinion wars."

Q: How do you handle a data-driven decision when
your manager disagrees with the data interpretation?

A: "I acknowledge the disagreement of interpretation
explicitly: 'I think the data supports X, and I
understand you read it as supporting Y. Can we
identify the model difference? You seem to be
weighting [metric A] more than I am. Is that right?'
This converts the disagreement from authority-based
to model-based. If the manager has contextual
information I don't have, they often reveal it
at this point. If they do not, I make my case with
the best reasoning I have and accept the outcome."

Q: Describe a time when no data was available and
you had to make a decision anyway.

A: "We needed to choose a message queue technology
for a new platform with no existing comparable
workload. No benchmark data was relevant. I defined
evaluation criteria: operational simplicity,
ecosystem maturity, our team's existing expertise,
and vendor support SLA. I scored three options
against these criteria and recommended the one that
scored highest. I was explicit that this was
judgment-under-uncertainty, not data-driven. I
documented what production data would validate or
invalidate the choice, and committed to a six-month
re-evaluation checkpoint."

#### Candidate Mistakes

Q: What do candidates get wrong about using data
in disagreements?

**What NOT to say:** "I showed them the benchmark
and they had to agree."

**Say instead:** "The data showed [specific result].
We had agreed upfront that this result would favor
approach A. We adopted approach A. The engineer
whose approach was not chosen said the process was
fair, even though the outcome was not their preference."

Q: How do candidates fail when data does not
clearly resolve the disagreement?

**What NOT to say:** "The data was mixed so we
just made a decision."

**Say instead:** "The data was ambiguous on [metric].
I proposed two interpretations with different
implications. We agreed on [interpretation] because
[reasoning] and documented the alternative in the
ADR to revisit if [condition] occurred."

Q: What is the data trap for false precision?

**What NOT to say:** "My benchmark showed it was
23.7% faster."

**Say instead:** "In my benchmark under these
conditions, approach A was consistently faster -
the difference was roughly 20-25%. Given the
variance in the measurements, I would not claim
a precise number, but the direction was clear."

Q: How do candidates use data as a weapon vs a tool?

**What NOT to say:** Present cherry-picked data
that supports only your position and call it
"data-driven."

**Say instead:** "I shared the data that supported
both positions and identified which data was most
relevant to our specific use case. I was explicit
about which evidence I was weighting and why."

#### Questions to Ask the Interviewer

Q: "How does this team make technical decisions
when there are multiple valid approaches?"

*Why:* Reveals whether the team has structured
decision-making or relies on authority or consensus.
*If asked back:* "I prefer a brief options doc with
explicit trade-offs, then a decision. The goal is
transparency in reasoning, not consensus on outcome."

Q: "What observability and benchmarking tools does
the team use to evaluate technical approaches?"

*Why:* Reveals the measurement infrastructure that
makes data-driven decisions possible.
*If asked back:* "I've used [tools] for benchmarking.
I particularly value tools that let you compare
two approaches under production-representative load."

Q: "How does the team handle it when a technical
decision turns out to be wrong six months later?"

*Why:* Reveals whether the team treats data as
a continuing evaluation or a one-time justification.
*If asked back:* "I try to document at the time of
a decision: what data would indicate this was wrong
and when we should revisit. This makes revisiting
a planned step, not a failure."

Q: "Can you describe a recent technical decision
the team made and how they evaluated the options?"

*Why:* The actual process reveals the culture better
than any stated values.
*If asked back:* "I would share how I'd approach
the same decision, which might reveal my framework
compared to theirs."
